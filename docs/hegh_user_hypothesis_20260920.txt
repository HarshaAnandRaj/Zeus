Hyperdimensional Exploration Geometry Hypothesis

Core hypothesis

A sufficiently high-dimensional normalized state space may make arbitrary state-to-state distances increasingly concentrated because of asymptotic orthogonality. This does not cause exploration. Rather, it may remove a geometric impediment to exploration by making the energetic cost of travel more predictable. Once that impediment is reduced, a comparatively small, independent exploration incentive may be sufficient to drive an agent away from locally profitable states without requiring strong repulsive dynamics or extreme reward pressure.

In compact form:

\boxed{
\text{High dimensionality}
\rightarrow
\text{asymptotic orthogonality}
\rightarrow
\text{distance concentration}
\rightarrow
\text{predictable travel cost}
\rightarrow
\text{reduced exploration barrier}
}

with exploration itself supplied separately:

\boxed{
\text{Exploration reward}
\rightarrow
\text{motivation to leave}
}

The combination, rather than either component alone, is the proposed mechanism.

⸻

1. Geometric basis

For approximately independent normalized vectors

x_i,x_j\in\mathbb R^D,

their inner product has approximately

E[x_i^\top x_j]=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(x_i^\top x_j)\propto\frac{1}{D}.

Therefore, as dimensionality increases,

x_i^\top x_j\rightarrow0,

and the Euclidean distance satisfies

\|x_i-x_j\|^2
=
2-2x_i^\top x_j
\rightarrow2.

Hence:

\|x_i-x_j\|\rightarrow\sqrt2.

The important property is not exact equidistance. It is concentration:

d_{ij}=\mu_d+\epsilon_{ij},

where relative variation in \epsilon_{ij} becomes increasingly small under the relevant assumptions.

Thus high dimensionality can produce a space in which arbitrary destinations remain separated while their distances become increasingly predictable.

⸻

2. The proposed exploration consequence

Consider an agent occupying a useful local state S, with possible destinations

F_1,F_2,\ldots,F_n.

In a geometry with highly variable travel distances,

d(S,F_1)\ll d(S,F_2)\ll d(S,F_3),

the agent experiences an implicit geometric preference.

Even if F_2 or F_3 could ultimately be valuable, exploration must overcome the additional cost of reaching them.

This creates a potential local concentration problem:

\text{local exploitation value}
+
\text{low local travel cost}
>
\text{uncertain remote value}
-
\text{high/variable travel cost}.

The agent can therefore remain concentrated around locally successful behavior even when exploration would be useful.

Under distance concentration:

d(S,F_i)\approx\mu_d

for generic candidate states.

This does not make Zeus explore.

It instead reduces the degree to which arbitrary geometric proximity privileges one candidate over another.

In other words:

\boxed{
\text{Equidistance/concentration is an affordance for exploration, not a cause of exploration.}
}

⸻

3. Predictable energetic expenditure

Suppose transition energy is related to travel distance:

C_{ij}=f(d_{ij}).

For approximately linear cost,

C_{ij}=k d_{ij}.

If distances concentrate,

\operatorname{Var}(d_{ij})\downarrow,

then, provided the transition dynamics preserve this relationship,

\operatorname{Var}(C_{ij})\downarrow.

Travel remains costly. The claim is not that high dimensionality makes movement free.

Instead:

\boxed{\text{energy loss from travel becomes more predictable}}

and therefore easier for a viability-sensitive controller to account for.

The agent no longer needs to solve as difficult a joint problem of:

\text{Should I explore?}
+
\text{How catastrophically expensive might this particular exploration be?}

The second term becomes more tightly bounded.

⸻

4. Exploration reward as the nudge

Distance concentration alone provides no reason to leave a known useful state.

Therefore a separate exploration incentive is required.

Let total behavioral value contain both viability/exploitation and exploration components:

R_{\text{total}}
=
R_{\text{viability}}
+
\lambda R_{\text{explore}}.

Here, \lambda controls exploration pressure.

In a heterogeneous geometry, a small \lambda may be overwhelmed by large differences in travel cost.

In a concentrated geometry, candidate transition costs may lie within a much narrower range. The exploration reward therefore needs primarily to overcome the decision to remain, rather than a potentially extreme and destination-specific geometric penalty.

The hypothesis is consequently not:

D\uparrow\Rightarrow\text{exploration}\uparrow.

It is:

\boxed{
D\uparrow
\Rightarrow
\text{distance dispersion}\downarrow
\Rightarrow
\text{travel-cost uncertainty}\downarrow
\Rightarrow
\text{small exploration pressure may become behaviorally effective}.
}

That distinction is essential.

⸻

5. Relationship to E1-B

E1-B showed that scarcity pressure alone substantially altered behavior.

Scarcity-trained controllers:

* travelled more,
* crossed between resources more frequently even after normalization by lifetime,
* inspected less,
* spent somewhat less energy,
* and survived longer,

yet all failed sustained finite-resource maintenance.

Therefore scarcity supplied some pressure to leave local exploitation, but did not produce economical, sustainable coordination.

The Hyperdimensional Exploration Geometry Hypothesis proposes a different factor:

The difficulty may not reside solely in providing a reason to explore. The representational/dynamical geometry through which exploration occurs may itself impose heterogeneous costs that make exploration difficult to regulate.

This does not reinterpret E1-B as evidence that hyperdimensionality is required. E1-B did not test this mechanism.

It merely motivates asking the question.

⸻

6. Important distinction: representational distance vs actual transition cost

This is the most important uncertainty.

Asymptotic orthogonality concerns geometry.

It does not guarantee:

C(S\rightarrow F_i)\approx C(S\rightarrow F_j).

Two representations can satisfy

\|S-F_i\|\approx\|S-F_j\|

while recurrent dynamics make one transition easy and the other extremely difficult.

Attractors, policy dynamics, action sequences, barriers, learned manifolds and environmental constraints can all recreate locality.

Therefore the critical bridge is:

\boxed{
\text{distance concentration}
\stackrel{?}{\longrightarrow}
\text{transition-cost concentration}.
}

If this bridge fails, the proposed exploration benefit does not follow merely from hyperdimensional geometry.

⸻

7. Why this differs from old Zeus repulsion

Earlier Zeus experiments attempted to maintain state separation through explicit dynamical repulsion.

That produced pathological expansion/wall-riding behavior rather than useful organization.

The present hypothesis is fundamentally different.

Repulsion says:

\text{actively push states apart}.

Hyperdimensional concentration asks whether:

\text{the geometry itself can provide abundant separation}

without continuously injecting outward dynamical force.

Thus the deeper architectural question becomes:

\boxed{
\text{Can dimensional geometry provide state separation without destructive dynamical repulsion?}
}

If so, exploration pressure could operate as a relatively small selection force, rather than simultaneously being responsible for preventing representational collapse.

⸻

8. Virtual hyperdimensionality as the first test

True hyperdimensional neural state is an expensive commitment.

A dense recurrent transformation can scale approximately as

O(D^2),

and merely increasing nominal dimensionality does not guarantee that the learned system uses those dimensions. Zeus could possess 10,000 coordinates while occupying a tiny effective manifold.

Therefore the geometric hypothesis should be tested before committing to a genuinely hyperdimensional controller.

A synthetic or virtual HD environment can reproduce the relevant distance statistics while retaining a small controller.

For example, construct candidate states whose pairwise similarities follow the statistics expected at virtual dimension D_v:

x_i^\top x_j
\sim
\mathcal N\left(0,\frac1{D_v}\right),

with corresponding distances

d_{ij}^2=2-2x_i^\top x_j.

Then vary

D_v=32,128,512,2048,8192,\ldots

without actually widening Zeus to those dimensions.

This isolates geometry from neural capacity.

⸻

9. Stronger experimental control

An even cleaner manipulation would hold mean travel distance constant while changing only dispersion:

E[d]=c

for every condition, while:

\operatorname{Var}(d):
\quad
\text{high}\rightarrow\text{medium}\rightarrow\text{low}.

This distinguishes:

“Exploration improves because travelling became cheaper”

from the actual hypothesis:

“Exploration improves because travel expenditure became more predictable and arbitrary geometric barriers diminished.”

A useful statistic would be:

CV_d=\frac{\sigma(d)}{E[d]}

and, separately,

CV_C=\frac{\sigma(C)}{E[C]}.

The hypothesis predicts the first should fall under simulated increasing dimensionality.

Whether the second follows is an empirical question, not an assumption.

⸻

10. Critical exploration pressure

A particularly useful dependent variable may be the minimum exploration incentive required to reliably escape persistent local exploitation:

\lambda^*=
\text{critical exploration pressure}.

Then ask how

\lambda^*

changes as distance dispersion decreases.

The strongest version of the hypothesis predicts that concentrated geometry should make exploration possible with a smaller or more stable incentive, without requiring destructive novelty seeking.

But simply increasing movement is insufficient.

Successful exploration should ultimately be distinguished from:

* random wandering,
* high action entropy,
* cyclic movement,
* novelty chasing,
* wasteful state switching,
* and movement that decreases viability.

The target is instrumentally useful exploration.

⸻

11. Falsification structure

This idea gives us several clean places where it can die:

\boxed{
D\uparrow
\rightarrow
\text{distance concentration}
\rightarrow
\text{transition-cost concentration}
\rightarrow
\text{exploration incentive becomes effective}
\rightarrow
\text{broader useful exploration}
\rightarrow
\text{improved adaptive control}
}

Every arrow is a separate claim.

If synthetic HD geometry fails to concentrate distances as intended, implementation fails.

If distances concentrate but transition costs do not, the geometry-to-economics hypothesis fails.

If transition costs concentrate but the same exploration reward produces no meaningful change, the reduced-barrier hypothesis fails.

If exploration increases but consists only of random wandering, the useful-exploration claim fails.

If useful exploration increases without improving viability/control, then exploration was not the limiting mechanism for the target behavior.

And if all of these work in virtual geometry but a true HD neural system collapses onto a low-dimensional manifold, then the remaining problem is learning/representation organization, not the geometric hypothesis itself.

⸻

Working hypothesis

Hyperdimensional Exploration Geometry Hypothesis (HEGH):
In a normalized sufficiently high-dimensional state space, asymptotic orthogonality can cause pairwise distances among generic states to concentrate around a narrow range. If actual transition costs inherit enough of this concentration, exploration between candidate states becomes energetically more predictable and less dominated by arbitrary geometric proximity. This does not itself generate exploration; rather, it removes or reduces a geometric barrier against it. A separate, modest exploration incentive may then be sufficient to destabilize persistent local exploitation and promote broader state-space sampling without requiring strong repulsive dynamics. The hypothesis can initially be tested using virtual hyperdimensional geometry while holding controller capacity and mean travel cost fixed, thereby separating the proposed geometric mechanism from increased neural capacity, cheaper travel, or additional compute.
